CHAPTER 2
REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES

2.1 Local and Foreign Studies/Literature

This chapter presents the related literature and studies that support the development of CCIS-CodeHub as an upgraded AI-driven academic platform for the College of Computing and Information Sciences. The review is organized according to the major concerns identified in Chapter 1, namely tool fragmentation, career-readiness gaps, limited peer-supported knowledge sharing, AI dependence in programming education, academic integrity in digital assessment, and the need to evaluate overall system performance. Consistent with the detailed writing guide, this chapter combines recent literature and studies under thematic areas and ends with a synthesis that identifies the research gap addressed by the thesis.

Integrated Digital Learning Ecosystems and Tool Fragmentation

One of the strongest foundations of the present study is the continuing fragmentation of digital academic work. In many higher education settings, students must move across separate tools for learning materials, communication, coding practice, project coordination, and assessment. This creates workflow discontinuity and increases unnecessary mental switching. In software environments, fragmentation has already been shown to complicate operational processes and increase overhead [1]. More recent educational literature similarly shows that digital competence in higher education is best developed within a learning ecosystem that integrates virtual learning environments, digital tools, and pedagogical approaches rather than treating them as isolated systems [7].

Nkomo, Daniel, and Butson likewise found that student engagement with digital technologies depends on how learners interact cognitively, behaviorally, and emotionally with their digital environment [8]. Their findings are important because they suggest that educational technologies become more effective when they support coherent, sustained participation rather than disconnected academic tasks. Skulmowski and Xu further explain that poorly structured online environments can produce extraneous cognitive load when learners are forced to divide attention across multiple interfaces and fragmented processes [9]. From a technical-work perspective, Goncales, Farias, and da Silva show that cognitive load remains a major concern in software engineering work involving analysis, programming, and comprehension [5], while Abbad-Andaloussi notes that understandability and cognitive load are deeply affected by how complex or fragmented technical artifacts are [22].

These studies support the first objective of the thesis: to unify learning and practice inside a coherent environment. They also justify the platform philosophy behind CCIS-CodeHub as a student experience platform rather than a single-purpose classroom tool.

Career Readiness, Employability, and Practice-Oriented Learning

Another major concern in the study is the gap between academic work and professional readiness. Computing students are expected not only to understand technical concepts, but also to communicate, collaborate, manage tasks, and work within iterative development environments. Yong and Ling found that soft-skill mismatch remains a significant concern in graduate employability [2]. This is especially relevant to computing students because technical competence alone does not fully prepare them for workplace expectations.

At the broader labor-market level, Jerman Blazic argues that fast-changing technology sectors continue to experience skill shortages and therefore require educational concepts capable of preparing learners more effectively for dynamic digital work [6]. This means that academic systems should not function only as repositories of content. They should also cultivate habits associated with real technical practice.

The literature on Agile-oriented instruction strengthens this argument. Poe and Mew found that Agile methodology can serve as a meaningful instructional format in software development courses because it supports iterative learning, collaboration, and more authentic task organization [11]. For the present thesis, this supports the inclusion of project workspaces, GitHub-linked collaboration, coding activities, and task workflows as educational features rather than merely technical additions.

Community, Peer Support, and Democratized Knowledge Sharing

Academic learning in computing is often strengthened by interaction with peers. However, useful knowledge frequently remains trapped inside isolated class sections, temporary group chats, or informal circles. This leaves many students dependent on delayed instructor response or disconnected social channels outside the academic environment. The literature suggests that this is not only a communication problem, but also a learning-structure problem.

Nkomo, Daniel, and Butson emphasize that digital engagement is shaped by the quality of participation surrounding academic tasks [8]. In addition, Le, Sok, and Heng found in their systematic review that peer mentoring in higher education improves academic performance, retention, emotional well-being, and social integration [10]. These findings justify the third objective of the thesis, which is to democratize knowledge sharing through a community-based digital ecosystem. In the context of CCIS-CodeHub, a community feed, peer discussion space, organization-level collaboration, and support exchange are academically meaningful because they reduce isolation and improve access to guidance within the same environment where learning and project work already occur.

Responsible AI Mentorship, Curriculum-Aware Guidance, and Fine-Tuned Academic Support

Generative AI has become one of the most influential developments in programming education. Current research shows that AI can help learners through explanation, tutoring support, debugging guidance, and code-related feedback, but it can also create dependency if used without instructional safeguards. Sadat, Ahmed, and Jony found that ChatGPT can facilitate the acquisition of fundamental programming skills for novices when it is used as structured educational support rather than as a shortcut [3]. Sun, Boudouaia, Zhu, and Li likewise found that ChatGPT-facilitated programming changes student behaviors, perceptions, and interaction patterns in measurable ways, showing that AI already affects how programming is learned and practiced [4].

Phung et al. further demonstrated that modern generative models can act as explainers, repair assistants, and tutoring aids in programming education, although they still vary in quality across different scenarios [12]. These findings support the educational value of AI mentorship, but they also make clear that the design of the AI layer matters.

This is where curriculum-aware and fine-tuned support become important. Liu et al. showed that retrieval-augmented and locally deployable language-model approaches can support computing students while improving contextual relevance and control over knowledge sources [13]. Li et al. similarly explain that retrieval-augmented generation improves factual grounding and dynamic knowledge updating in educational applications by linking outputs to external knowledge sources [14]. For the present thesis, these studies justify moving beyond generic AI chat toward an AI mentor shaped by course context, institutional materials, pedagogical prompting, and a fine-tuning-ready academic architecture.

Academic Integrity, Vision-Based Proctoring, and Digital Assessment

The rise of online and hybrid learning has made academic integrity a central issue in digital assessment. Holden, Norris, and Kuhlmeier found that online assessment requires deliberate integrity safeguards rather than simple transfer of traditional paper-based practices into digital settings [16]. More recent work reinforces this point. Alguacil et al. reported that monitoring conditions in online examinations can materially affect dishonest behavior and assessment outcomes [17]. Nigam et al., in their systematic review of AI-based proctoring systems, showed that modern proctoring architectures increasingly rely on automated detection of suspicious behaviors such as face absence, multiple persons, or other anomalies, while also raising important privacy and ethical questions [18].

The academic-integrity discussion has become even more urgent under generative AI. Ateeq et al. argue that AI is reshaping assessment, academic honesty, and evaluation culture, requiring institutions to move toward more deliberate, policy-aware, and holistic assessment design [15]. This body of work directly supports the fifth objective of the thesis: to implement advanced vision-based integrity mechanisms within the platform. It also supports the argument that integrity should be treated as a layered framework combining assessment controls, monitoring, behavior analysis, and accountable AI-assisted proctoring flow.

System Evaluation and Performance Assessment

The upgraded scope of the thesis also requires a formal basis for evaluating system performance. The sixth specific objective of the study, which focuses on evaluating system performance, needs to be supported not only by feature-based discussion but also by recognized software-quality standards. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 provides a formal product quality model that includes characteristics such as functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability, reliability, security, maintainability, flexibility, and safety [20]. This framework is appropriate for CCIS-CodeHub because the thesis is not limited to proposing features; it is also concerned with whether the integrated platform performs in a stable, usable, secure, and efficient way.

In addition, the reviewed literature on digital learning environments and AI-supported systems implies that performance should not be understood narrowly as speed alone. In this thesis, performance also involves system responsiveness during quizzes and real-time monitoring, stability of project and community modules, usability of integrated flows, relevance of AI-generated guidance, and the overall reliability of the platform under academic use.

2.2 Synthesis of the Review

The reviewed literature leads to several clear conclusions. First, recent studies support the need for integrated digital ecosystems rather than fragmented sets of disconnected tools [1], [7], [8], [9], [5], [22]. Second, employability and career readiness in computing depend not only on technical instruction, but also on environments that cultivate collaboration, task management, and authentic software-development practice [2], [6], [11]. Third, peer-supported knowledge sharing and structured student interaction remain valuable because they improve engagement, belonging, and access to academic assistance [8], [10]. Fourth, AI can improve programming education when it is guided, contextual, and pedagogically framed, but it can also reshape student behavior in ways that require careful academic design [3], [4], [12], [13], [14], [15]. Fifth, digital assessment increasingly requires integrity-aware design and proctoring-aware monitoring frameworks [15], [16], [17], [18]. Sixth, a thesis-level system must also be evaluated using a recognized quality framework such as ISO/IEC 25010 [20].

Despite these developments, a gap remains. Many existing educational platforms still address only a narrow portion of the student experience. Some provide content delivery without collaboration. Others support teamwork without curricular guidance. Public AI tools may provide help but remain disconnected from course context and academic accountability. Assessment tools may support quizzes but lack integrity-aware monitoring. This fragmentation reveals a clear opportunity for a platform designed specifically for computing students and structured around their learning, project work, community interaction, AI-assisted support, integrity requirements, and measurable performance.

Hence, this study proposes CCIS-CodeHub as an integrated, AI-driven, curriculum-aware, and integrity-aware academic platform for the College of Computing and Information Sciences. The next chapter presents the theoretical framework, conceptual framework, technical background, and system requirements that guided the design of the proposed platform.
